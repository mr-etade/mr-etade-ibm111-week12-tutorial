Linear Independence of Vectors

When does a vector add something new — and which test should you use to find out?

Welcome. Last week you saw how vectors combine. This week, you decide whether a set of vectors is linearly independent (every vector adds something new) or linearly dependent (at least one is redundant). Three test methods are available — the trick is choosing the right one. Use the four demos below to build that intuition, then move on to the practice questions.

Demo 2

Spotting Dependence at a Glance

Some vector sets are obviously dependent — before you even pick up a pen. Train your eye on the four patterns below. Click each card to reveal the verdict and the reason.

Pattern A · Parallel vectors

$\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$

Click to reveal · one is a scalar multiple of the other

Dependent. Notice $\begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 6 \end{bmatrix} = 2\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$. Whenever one vector is a scalar multiple of another, the set is dependent — no calculation needed.

Pattern B · Contains the zero vector

$\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$

Click to reveal · what does the zero vector contribute?

Dependent. Take $c_1 = 1, c_2 = c_3 = 0$ to get $1 \cdot \vec{0} + 0 \cdot \vec{v}_2 + 0 \cdot \vec{v}_3 = \vec{0}$ — a non-trivial solution. Any set containing $\vec{0}$ is dependent.

Pattern C · More vectors than dimensions

$\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 5 \\ 7 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$

Click to reveal · three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$

Dependent. You cannot fit more than $n$ independent vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$. With 3 vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$, the third must be a combination of the first two (here: $5\vec{v}_1 + 7\vec{v}_2$).

Pattern D · Standard unit vectors

$\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$

Click to reveal · the cleanest example

Independent. Each vector points along a different axis — none can be combined from the others. This is the standard basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$, and the foundation of every coordinate system.

Pattern E · A repeated vector

$\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 4 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$

Click to reveal · spot the repeat

Dependent. Take $c_1 = 1, c_2 = 0, c_3 = -1$: $\,1 \cdot \vec{v}_1 + 0 \cdot \vec{v}_2 - 1 \cdot \vec{v}_1 = \vec{0}.$ Any set with a repeated vector is dependent.

Pattern F · No obvious shortcut

$\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix},\ \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 5 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$

Click to reveal · not parallel, no zeros, just-right size

Needs a method. No quick-spot pattern applies. Use the determinant: $\det\!\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 5 \end{bmatrix} = 5 - 6 = -1 \ne 0$, so the set is independent. When in doubt, compute.
